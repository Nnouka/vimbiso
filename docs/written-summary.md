# Vimbiso — Written Summary (SUB-3)

**Track:** Safety, Reporting & Protection, with a Transparency & Accountability cross-track element via Pattern Watch.
**Pilot geography:** Kenya. **Primary channel:** WhatsApp (USSD is out of scope for this 10-day build; see Roadmap).
**Team:** Tendai Moyo (product, pitch, reviewer coordination) and Nnouka (full-stack build; French-language lead), building with Claude Code as a genuine AI pair-programmer throughout.

---

## 1. The problem, and why this is trustworthy information

Kenya documented 220 femicides in 2025, 129 of them in the first quarter alone — roughly one every three days becoming one nearly every day. 1,069 women were killed between 2016 and 2025, with boyfriend-perpetrated killings rising sharply. These figures are corroborated across the Heinrich Böll Foundation, Africa Uncensored / Africa Data Hub, UN Women, and UNESCO — not a single source, and not our own estimate.

The more urgent fact for a hackathon on trustworthy information: **the Kenyan government stopped publishing femicide data after March 2025.** That is a live transparency failure happening now, not a historical statistic. A tool that responds to individual reports of danger, honestly labeled and independently verifiable, matters more in exactly this kind of information vacuum than it would if official reporting were intact.

## 2. Why Kenya, stated as a real comparison

Zimbabwe, Cameroon, Rwanda, and Tanzania were also considered, scored against: severity/currency of crisis data, existing institutional infrastructure to route survivors into, language fit, and hackathon-community reach. Kenya won on three concrete grounds: the strongest and most multi-sourced current data of the five; a real, UN-backed, actively-promoted national helpline (HAK/1195) that makes Vimbiso's "connect me to a counsellor" step a genuine warm handoff rather than a mocked-up promise; and an actual participating Andela hackathon chapter (@KEN), which turned translation-reviewer sourcing into a concrete channel instead of cold outreach.

Cameroon was seriously considered and rejected for a specific, honest reason: its own femicide data is what forced a correction to Vimbiso's core safety mechanism during development (see §4), but Human Rights Watch's account of Cameroon's institutional weakness means there may not yet be a reliable "door" for a smart front-end like Vimbiso to stand in front of. That's a market-readiness problem no amount of good engineering fixes — so Cameroon (and Rwanda) are named as Phase 2 targets, not overclaimed as pilot markets, reflecting where our own team has real, not aspirational, standing.

## 3. Trust and accuracy approach

Three commitments run through the whole build, and each is backed by something checkable, not just a claim:

**No survivor or perpetrator is ever named in the data model.** `reports` and `trusted_contacts` have no legal-name, name, or id-number column — confirmed by direct schema inspection, not just design intent. A perpetrator identifier, when a survivor optionally chooses to submit one for cross-report pattern detection, is salted-hashed (HMAC-SHA256) before it ever touches storage; a live audit this sprint searched every text and jsonb column in every table of a real, exercised database for the raw test identifier used in testing and found zero matches outside the one column meant to hold its hash.

**Every cross-report or outbound action requires explicit, logged consent.** `perpetrator_consent_given` and `wants_counsellor_connect` both default to false; live database queries confirm no hash row and no SMS alert exists for any report where the survivor didn't actually opt in — including a HIGH-risk report that never tapped "connect me now," which correctly generated no SMS despite its risk level.

**Every claim about what's built is labeled by how real it is, not rounded up.** Content strings carry an explicit tier (`FULL` / `PARTIAL` / `ARCHITECTURE_ONLY`); English is the only `FULL` language (87 keys), Swahili and French are `PARTIAL` and explicitly unreviewed (11 keys each — only the safety-critical high-risk sequence and the Pattern Watch consent prompt), and the code logs a fallback to English rather than silently showing untranslated placeholder text. Directory entries (hotline numbers, addresses) are labeled in the seed file with the actual confidence level behind each one, and are explicitly flagged as not yet spot-checked against a live source by a human — a task no AI agent in this sandbox could do without real internet access to call and verify.

**Anonymous by default, but not anonymous forever if the law requires otherwise — designed explicitly, not left implicit.** A message-analysis log (`docs/backlog.md`'s LOG epic; built and verified against a real database) groups a sender's messages by a one-way HMAC pseudonym that no key this application holds can ever reverse — day-to-day, no one, including our own engineers, can turn a logged conversation back into a phone number. But a safety tool that may face a lawful subpoena or government request cannot pretend that anonymity is absolute either, so a SEPARATE, deliberately harder-to-reach mechanism (a small `sender_identity_map`, encrypted under its own key held out-of-band by a designated custodian rather than living in the app's normal runtime secrets, decryptable only by a standalone script never imported into the running application) allows lawful re-identification of one sender at a time — and every time that capability is actually used, it writes to an append-only `identity_recovery_log`, so re-identifying a survivor is never silent, even when it's justified. This is a genuine design tradeoff, stated plainly rather than picking a side and hiding the other: pure anonymity would leave the organization unable to comply with a lawful order; a normal per-message real identifier would leave survivors with no privacy at all day-to-day. The two-tier design is our answer to that tension. What remains genuinely undecided — not an implementation gap, a real open policy question — is who the designated key custodian is in practice, what counts as a valid lawful request, and how long logged conversations are retained at all.

## 4. How the risk-response mechanism works, and how it changed

Every report runs through a fixed 8-question risk screen (documented lethality-risk factors, answered via tappable buttons, never typed sensitive detail). Strangulation alone is a hard override to HIGH risk, independent of anything else; four or more of a second risk-factor cluster crosses a threshold to HIGH; otherwise the report is STANDARD. A HIGH-risk score fires an automatic, four-part response in the same handling cycle as the 8th answer — no further input required, no second party needed to act first: an immediate safety plan, the real hotline number (queried live from the resource directory, never hardcoded), a counsellor-connect option that alerts an on-call number by SMS containing no survivor-identifying information, and — if the survivor registered one in advance — a vague, coded check-in message to a trusted contact.

This design is not the one we started with. The original concept required a second, independent report naming the same perpetrator before a case became visible — a "matching-escrow" model built to prevent false accusations, modeled loosely on a real Cameroon femicide pattern. Stress-testing that mechanism against the actual Cameroon evidence surfaced a fatal flaw: real cases show a first report can be the last chance to intervene, and a system that waits for corroboration before responding to danger is unsafe by construction. The mechanism was redesigned mid-build to risk-triage-first, with cross-report pattern-matching demoted to a separate, non-urgent, consent-gated "Pattern Watch" feature that never gates or delays a survivor's own safety response. This pivot — using real-world evidence to catch a safety-critical flaw in our own design before building further on it — is the single most consequential decision in this project's history, and is documented in full in `docs/ai-tool-usage-log.md`, Phase 2.

## 5. AI-tool usage — specific, not general

The honest, demonstrable claim from this project's history is not "we used AI a lot." It's: **AI was used to generate and stress-test multiple real options fast, and every safety-critical or trust-critical output was still gated by explicit human verification or, where a human couldn't verify directly, by the strongest automated verification this sandboxed environment could produce.** Specifics:

- **Test-driven development for safety/privacy-critical logic.** The risk-scoring function and the perpetrator-hashing utility were both built test-first: a human/AI-agent pairing wrote failing tests against the documented rules before the implementation existed, then implemented against them. The same discipline was applied again in Sprint 2 for the Pattern Watch hash-matching logic (13 test cases written before the function existed, independently re-run against the real implementation rather than trusting a self-report).
- **A simulated, role-specialized AI team.** Each sprint was built by multiple AI agents in fixed roles (Product Manager, Designer, QA, Backend, Networking, Frontend) working from a PM-authored plan with frozen interface contracts agreed before implementation — deliberately mirroring how a real small team avoids integration friction, and catching real cross-agent disagreements (a duplicate function definition, a content-key mismatch) before they shipped.
- **Translation drafting, always gated by disclosed review status.** Swahili and French safety-critical copy was AI-drafted, then explicitly seeded as `PARTIAL`/unreviewed rather than silently upgraded — Swahili pending a volunteer reviewer from the @KEN hackathon channel, French committed to a real review by Nnouka, a native French speaker and core team member, not yet completed as of this submission.
- **Progressively stronger automated verification as sandbox capabilities were discovered.** Sprint 1 and 2 verified logic via hand-rolled Jest-compatible harnesses (`npm install` is blocked by this sandbox's network policy). Mid-build, a global TypeScript compiler was discovered to be already present despite that block, enabling a real `tsc --noEmit`-equivalent check that caught one genuine bug (a duplicate object-literal key that would have failed a real typecheck) no amount of manual review had caught across two agents' edits to the same file. Sprint 3 discovered a second capability — a pre-installed local PostgreSQL 16 — and built real (not type-only) runtime shims routing the `pg` driver through the actual `psql` CLI, letting the project's real, unmodified code run against a real database across 5 full conversation journeys. That verification found zero product defects but five real bugs in the verification harness itself, each root-caused and fixed before its output was trusted as evidence — documented in full, including the specific bugs, in `docs/ai-tool-usage-log.md`'s Sprint 3 entry.
- **What AI did not do:** verify a phone number or URL against the real world (no live internet access in this build sandbox — flagged explicitly wherever it matters, e.g. the resource directory), send a real SMS or complete a real Twilio WhatsApp Sandbox round-trip (no outbound network access to Twilio's API), or sign off a translation as reviewed-accurate (translation review requires a fluent human; the tier system exists specifically so AI-drafted content is never mistaken for reviewed content).

## 6. What's real vs. simulated, stated plainly

**Real:** the full conversation state machine, risk scoring, and hashing logic, running as actual code; a real local Postgres database, actually migrated, seeded, and exercised through 5 full conversation journeys; live SQL-level verification of every dashboard query the counsellor-facing tool depends on; a genuine privacy audit run against that live, exercised data; and the pseudonymous message-analysis log plus its lawful-re-identification mechanism described above — `message_log` is written on every real inbound/outbound send, `sender_identity_map`/`identity_recovery_log` are written and read by a real, standalone recovery script, and a full HIGH-risk journey was driven end-to-end against a real database to confirm all of it, including a correct plaintext round-trip through the encryption.

**Still simulated, and stated as such rather than glossed over:** no live Twilio WhatsApp Sandbox round-trip has happened (this sandboxed dev environment has no outbound access to Twilio's API); no real SMS or WhatsApp message has reached a real phone — the send code path is real and independently verified, the network call itself is captured, not transmitted; the counsellor dashboard's actual Express/EJS page rendering has not been run or screenshotted (no real `express`/`ejs` install available here — substituted with direct verification of the dashboard's exact SQL queries against real data, which is strong evidence about correctness but is not the same thing as a screenshot); the on-call counsellor number is a single demo phone number, not a live integration agreement with HAK/1195 or any real Kenyan GBV organization; Swahili/French translations are AI-drafted and not yet reviewed by a fluent speaker; and who the real-world designated key custodian is, what counts as a valid lawful request, and how long logged conversations are retained are genuine, undecided policy questions — the mechanism to act on those decisions is built and verified, the organizational decisions themselves are not this codebase's to make.

Full evidence for every claim above — real query output, not just assertions — is in `docs/qa-sprint3-report.md` and `docs/qa-script.md`.
